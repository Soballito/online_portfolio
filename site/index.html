Personal Information

01About

I'm Nico Soballa, a mechanical engineer and product designer from Dortmund, and an enthusiast for design, innovation and technology. Today I work in the Innovation – Smart City team at the Economic Development Agency of the City of Dortmund.

I studied mechanical engineering at RWTH Aachen University, where I earned my Bachelor of Science, then moved to Denmark for a Master of Science in IT Product Design at SDU Kolding, which I completed with distinction. Since then I have gathered more than five years of experience between engineering, design and project management: in research and development at HILTI, in prototype development at LEGO, as a product design engineer at THE ARC and as a research associate at the Institute for Fire and Rescue Technology in Dortmund.

Whatever the project, I focus on the people behind it: the ones who use a product, build it or depend on it. I am communicative, creative and confident, and when a project needs something I don't know yet, I teach it to myself. Outside of work I am always on the lookout for new adventures and hopelessly addicted to sport, from surfing, skating and snowboarding to CrossFit and pretty much everything else.

IFR Dortmund, Dräger, TU Dortmund, WHZ Zwickau, IdF NRW Münster

Firefighter in full breathing apparatus during a smoke test · Source: IFR

Firefighters advancing through flames with almost no visibility

Ultra-wideband localisation module mounted on the protective gear

Mask display: team members A, B and C with distances, remaining time, air pressure and unit

Presenting the CelidonPRO demonstrator to visitors at a trade fair

CelidonPRO shows firefighters where their team is when smoke takes away everything else. Ultra-wideband modules built into the protective gear locate every crew member without any infrastructure, and a display inside the breathing mask shows direction and distance in real time. I managed the project for the Dortmund Fire Department's Institute for Fire and Rescue Technology, shaped the product and interface design of the demonstrator, and kept the firefighters' needs at the centre of every decision.

Inside a burning building, visibility can drop to zero within seconds. Crews lose sight of each other, orientation fades, and when someone goes missing nobody knows where to look, while the air in a breathing apparatus lasts only around twenty minutes. Existing tracking relies on beacons or infrastructure that simply isn't there at an unfamiliar fire scene. The task was to build a localisation system that works ad hoc, anywhere, and that firefighters would trust and actually use under stress, in thick gloves and full protective equipment.

The project brought together Dräger as consortium lead, TU Dortmund, Westsächsische Hochschule Zwickau, the Institut der Feuerwehr NRW in Münster and our institute, funded by the Federal Ministry of Research, Technology and Space. I managed the project in close coordination with all partners, keeping research, engineering and fire service practice aligned. To find out what the product really needed, I planned and facilitated workshops and user tests with firefighters, then turned what we learned into requirements, product concepts and interface designs for the demonstrator.

The interface had to say a lot with almost nothing. In the mask display every element competes with the firefighter's real view, so we reduced it to what matters in the moment: where my team members are, how far away, and in which direction. Each iteration went back to the people who would wear it, tested in full gear and low visibility, until the design worked without explanation.

The project closed with a live demonstration of the integrated system under realistic operating conditions: firefighters in full gear, zero visibility, and their crew's positions visible inside the mask. Along the way I presented the project and its results at trade fairs, showing how research becomes equipment that firefighters actually want to use.

THE ARC, NOTO DESIGN, GECK, BVB, GLS

BACE HUB: collect online orders, drop off parcels, take home local products · Source: THE ARC

The first BACE HUB at the Westfalenstadion, running a BVB × GLS raffle on its screen

Ideation workshop with modelling clay, sticky notes and a lot of questions

Electronic compartment lock, cable routing through the frame and the latch controller

Match day at the first BACE HUB with GLS and BVB

BACE HUB is a smart pickup station: one place to collect online orders, drop off parcels and take home local products, opened with a quick scan of a QR code. I was part of the founding team, and together with one colleague I was responsible for everything hardware: research, design, development and production management, all the way to our first physical hub at the Westfalenstadion in Dortmund, built in cooperation with BVB and GLS.

A start-up has no time to spare, and a hub is not a box you simply put down. It needs a licence for its location, a reliable electrical supply and the approval of everyone who owns, runs or uses the space around it. Our first hub stood in front of one of the busiest stadiums in Germany, which meant permits, power connections and a growing circle of stakeholders to coordinate, while the product itself was still being invented.

It started with ideas. Together with our partner at the time, NOTO DESIGN, I planned and held workshops to explore what a hub should be, how people would approach it and how it should feel to use. From there I carried the concepts into hardware: researching components, designing the construction and preparing it for production.

Then it got physical. I managed the manufacturing at GECK and built, wired and tested the hub myself: an electronic lock in every compartment, cables routed through the frame, and the controller that connects each door to the digital interface. On site at the stadium I was also the handyman who kept it running. It showed me how tightly the physical product, the user interface and the overall feeling belong together: a door that opens a second too late is a user experience problem.

The first BACE HUB opened at the Westfalenstadion and met the fans on match days, with a BVB × GLS raffle running on its screen. For me it was a crash course in project and product management and in how hardware products are really developed, from the first idea to a working station in public space.

ABUS, THE ARC

Two padlocks on one container door: everyday access control on a construction site

Key cabinets, chains and padlocks: how access is managed on construction sites today

Workshop board with use cases from the site visits, from containers to key cabinets

On-site research in a building under construction, and an early smart lock prototype kit

The fair stand presenting the ABUS smart lock system

SMART LOCK began with a question from ABUS: what could a smart lock solution for construction sites look like? I managed the project on behalf of THE ARC, from the first workshops with the ABUS team to research on real construction sites, interviews, prototype tests and a physical trade fair stand presenting the ABUS smart lock system.

Construction sites are tough on products and on people. Many trades share one site, equipment and access change hands every day, and every key handed out is one more to keep track of. ABUS approached us with the idea of a smart lock solution for exactly this environment. The real question behind the brief was whether the people on site actually needed it, and what it would have to be for them to use it.

I prepared and facilitated workshops with the ABUS team to shape the idea and make our assumptions visible. Then I went where the product would live: onto construction sites, for product research, interviews and hands-on experience. I designed and ran prototype tests with the people who would use such a system every day, and co-created a physical fair stand to present the ABUS smart lock system.

My motto: prototype quickly to find out whether you are on the right track. I would rather put a rough prototype into someone's hands on site than develop something for months and publish it without ever talking to the real customers. Watching how people actually work often shows that what they wanted is far simpler than your idea.

The biggest learning: not every project has to end in a product to be worth it. The on-site research, interviews and prototype tests showed that the need for such a product on construction sites was not there yet. ABUS still uses smart locks, but the construction sector is currently no longer one of its main target groups. Finding that out early, before building at scale, is exactly what research is for.

THE ARC, jojorama, ecom

An ecom analyser next to a competitor's device: comparing interfaces and handling

Getting into the technology: an opened-up measuring device at ecom

Interfaces in the field today: a competitor's menu and an ecom measurement screen

GAS DETECTOR was a product development project for ecom, a manufacturer of exhaust gas analysers and measuring technology from Iserlohn, whose devices are used by heating technicians and chimney sweeps. I managed the project on behalf of THE ARC: from working my way into gas measurement and its market, through interviews and observations with users, to more than ten prototypes with the Hannover design team jojorama and a new user interface for ecom.

Gas measurement was new to me, so the first job was to learn it properly: how the devices work, what they have to measure, and how the market around ecom and its competitors is structured. A good device here is not only accurate. It has to fit into the working day of people who carry it from one heating system to the next and use it in cramped, dusty corners.

I facilitated interviews with customers and watched them work with ecom devices and with products from other manufacturers. I observed the interaction, the handling and the whole product system around it: where the devices are kept, how they are transported and in which environments they are used. Together with jojorama, a design team based in Hannover, we turned these insights into more than ten prototypes and narrowed them down to a few through user tests with 3D-printed models.

I also led the team that developed the new user interface for ecom. On a device that is held in one hand in the middle of a job, every menu level and every button press counts, so the interface had to follow the way technicians actually work rather than the structure of the software behind it. The prototypes and detailed concepts remain confidential, which is why this page shows the research rather than the results.

For reasons I am not allowed to talk about, the project was stopped and all the work went into ecom's shelves. What stayed with me is this: at any point in a project, something can happen that overturns all the ideas and implementation you have discussed for so long, and sometimes it all depends on a single person.

THE ARC, jojorama, Sprenger

The AERO safety stirrup in use · Source: Sprenger

The flexible side element opens under the pressure of the foot

AERO in Sprenger's design language: anodised aluminium and a black flexible side

Tread with optional stainless steel spikes, and how the side element opens

AERO in the saddle

AERO is a safety stirrup by Sprenger. One side of the stirrup is a flexible element that keeps the foot in place while riding but opens under the pressure of the foot in a fall, so the rider is not dragged along. It was one of my first projects at THE ARC: I joined as the mechanical engineer, developing the safety system together with the design studio jojorama, and after a few months I also took on the role of project manager.

A fall from a horse is dangerous enough. It becomes far worse when a foot stays caught in the stirrup and the rider is dragged. The task was a release system that holds the foot securely in every riding situation, yet frees it reliably in the moment it matters, and that fits the precise, high-quality design language of a manufacturer like Sprenger.

We started with research on existing safety systems and on Sprenger itself: its mentality, its products and its visual design language. Together with jojorama we built prototypes to test our ideas, and because the Sprenger team includes passionate riders, we could do our prototype testing right on site.

The result is a stirrup in anodised aluminium with a movable side element that lets the foot release sideways, upwards, forwards or backwards. An extra-wide tread with optional stainless steel spikes gives grip, and a 5° incline supports the rider's leg position. My part as the mechanical engineer was the safety system: making the release work reliably while the stirrup stays strong and stable in everyday riding.

AERO is now on the market. For me it was the perfect example of how well three different stakeholders, Sprenger, jojorama and THE ARC, can work together: creatively, proactively and with a close eye on time management.

LEGO, LEGO MINDSTORMS team

A LEGO MINDSTORMS robot, the kind that played rock paper scissors with the children · Source: LEGO

At LEGO I started as an intern in the prototype development department, where every two weeks brought a new topic to ideate on and new prototypes to build and present to the team in charge. That led to my master's thesis with the LEGO MINDSTORMS team: how to implement machine learning into the LEGO MINDSTORMS environment, with a focus on the use of computer vision for children under the age of ten.

Machine learning usually hides behind data sets, code and abstract logic, far away from how children under ten play and learn. The question was how to make it tangible for them: a way into machine learning and programming that works through what they already have, their bodies, their play and their imagination.

During my time in the prototype development department, a new topic every two weeks taught me to ideate fast, build quickly and present prototypes to the people responsible for each topic. For my master's thesis on machine learning and computer vision in LEGO MINDSTORMS, I created a tool that used computer vision to recognise children's movements for rock, paper and scissors, and connected it to a LEGO MINDSTORMS robot that played against them.

First the children recorded their own movement for rock, paper and scissors, training the model themselves and discovering how a machine learns from examples. Then they pressed play and a real game began: the robot answered with a random choice, celebrated when it won and cried when it lost. What started as a game of rock paper scissors sometimes turned into a dancing session with the robot.

The thesis was a success, and I continued to work for LEGO for a while to bring what I had learned into the LEGO MINDSTORMS world. It taught me far more than coding and machine learning: especially with children, the result can differ widely from what you expect, and human–computer interaction is deeply individual, depending not only on age but on the person.

HILTI

Loading ceiling hangers into the DX 350 pole tool, one wire at a time

Setting ceiling hangers with the DX 350 pole tool in the test rig

The 3D-printed wire magazine on the pole

High-speed recording at 100,000 fps of a fastening into concrete, and a worn piston tip

Watching how the tool is really handled

At HILTI I worked as a mechanical engineer on two projects. The first was to create an accessory for the DX 350 ceiling tool, which fastens hangers with long wires into concrete ceilings. The second was to find solutions for a recurring failure of the piston tip inside HILTI's battery-actuated fastening tools. Both started the same way: with research, interviews and site visits to understand the real problems before touching a solution.

For the pole tool I went all the way back to the manufacturing of the ceiling hangers, to understand how they are made, loaded and set into the ceiling with the DX 350. The problem showed itself on site: every hanger comes with a long wire, and loading them one by one meant bending down again and again, hour after hour, a direct path to back pain.

The answer was a simple yet helpful invention: a magazine for the wires. Instead of bending down for every single hanger, users could load a few dozen wires at once and then work through them at the ceiling. I designed it, 3D-printed it and tested it myself in the test rig until it held the wires reliably and fitted the way people actually work with the pole.

The piston project was a different kind of detective work. I interviewed the HILTI teams working with the affected tools, then designed and facilitated workshops across departments to find where their problems overlapped. I tested the tools myself and made high-resolution slow-motion recordings to see what really happens in the fraction of a second the piston strikes. From there I developed ten improvement ideas, from changing the temperature during material manufacturing to two-material pistons or a new nail geometry.

Both projects gave me a great deal of mechanical engineering knowledge, while always keeping an eye on who uses the tools and how. My biggest takeaway: a common misuse of a tool that seems trivial to use can cause high costs for the updates that follow. Understanding the people behind the tool is part of engineering it.
